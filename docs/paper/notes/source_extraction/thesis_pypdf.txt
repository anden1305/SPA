Characterizing Sleep Patterns by use of Dynamic Unsupervised Machine Learning Approaches Andreas Holmer Bigom & Oliver Rosbæk Elmgreen MSc Thesis, DTU Compute Supervisors: Morten Mørup & Birgitte Rahbek Kornum February 2026

Abstract This report evaluates dynamic unsupervised sequence models for characterising mouse sleep stages from electroencephalography (EEG) and electromyography (EMG), with emphasis on transfer to unseen subjects. Controlled synthetic experiments validate optimisation behaviour and latent state recovery, and show that when stage information is primarily temporal, autoregressive emissions are required for discrimination. Models are then assessed on Mouse Sleep Staging Validation recordings using subject level ﬁtting and leave one subject out population prediction on a curated subset of ten mice, with agreement quantiﬁed by Normalised Mutual Information (NMI) against expert Wake, non- rapid eye movement sleep (NREM), and rapid eye movement sleep (REM) labels. Direct time domain modelling with Hidden Markov models and multivariate autoregressive variants shows negligible population level agreement. Expert engineered spectral and amplitude features restore meaningful unsupervised alignment but retain a generalisation gap under leave one subject out prediction, reaching population NMI of about 0.51. The most robust transfer is achieved by subject conditioned representation learning, where a conditional Gaussian mixture variational autoencoder preserves population predictive performance and yields the strongest agreement when its subject conditioned mixture prior is used directly for prediction, reaching population NMI of about 0.70. A complementary substage analysis shows that the cGMV AE mixture prior supports stable ﬁner grained states that preserve the Wake, NREM, and REM organisation while reﬁning transitions. These results indicate that subject conditioned latent clustering captures transferable sleep stage structure, enabling robust cross subject staging even before explicit temporal modelling. i

Characterizing Sleep Patterns by use of Dynamic Unsupervised Machine Learning Approaches 1 Introduction Sleep staging in the mouse o"ers a direct and quantitative lens on how brain states unfold over time, yet automated pipelines often lose reliability when applied to a new subject. This report assesses dynamic unsupervised sequence models that infer latent organisation from physiological recordings and examines whether the learned structure transfers robustly across subjects while remaining consistent with expert annotations. The analysis further evaluates whether the latent states admit stable and interpretable substages, thereby motivating the methodological and experimental developments presented throughout the remainder of the report. 1.1 Motivation Sleep is a major determinant of brain and systemic health, shaping processes that include memory consolidation and immune regulation [ 2, 3]. Deviations in sleep archi- tecture are sensitive indicators of neurological, psychiatric, and metabolic disorders [4, 5]. Robust methods that characterise sleep stage structure therefore support both mechanistic neuroscience and translational work that aims to connect physiology to measurable outcomes. Progress in these areas depends on more than total sleep time. Evidence suggests that learning and memory consolidation, mood regulation, and cardiometabolic risk relate to sleep composition and stage structure rather than to total sleep time alone [2, 5]. Sleep is also inherently temporal, since its architecture reﬂects how stages persist, fragment, and transition across cycles [ 6]. Methods that preserve stage dynamics and transitions therefore provide a quantitative signature of the processes that regulate sleep and wakefulness [7]. Mouse sleep o"ers a well controlled experimental model for studying brain rhythms and sleep regulation. Shared molecular pathways and conserved neural circuits enable ﬁndings from mice to inform hypotheses that can later be evaluated in humans [8]. Mouse studies can be scaled to larger cohorts at reasonable cost, and invasive recordings and targeted manipulations are feasible under controlled conditions [ 8, 9]. These properties make mouse sleep datasets an attractive foundation for building quantitative tools that can support mechanistic discovery. At the same time, murine sleep introduces modelling challenges that reward principled dynamic methods. Mice exhibit shorter and more fragmented sleep cycles than humans, which increases the density of transitions between Wake, non rapid eye movement sleep, and rapid eye movement sleep [ 9, 10]. Murine electroencephalography (EEG) is also commonly recorded with fewer electrodes and limited spatial coverage, which reduces spatial information and increases reliance on spectral content and temporal context. As a result, robust staging depends on representations that remain informative under limited channel coverage and across heterogeneous recordings. A further practical bottleneck is that standard staging pipelines can be imprecise and di!cult to scale. Variability across subjects and the presence of subtle, complex substages make understanding and characterising sleep dynamics challenging. Unsupervised sequence models provide a principled route to address these chal- lenges because they infer latent structure directly from observed EEG and electromyo- graphy (EMG) dynamics. Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) provide an interpretable probabilistic framework that aligns naturally with the organisation of sleep into discrete stages with characteristic persistence and transitions [ 1, 14]. Variational autoencoder based approaches learn compact non linear embeddings that can better capture latent structure in the underlying EEG and EMG dynamics [ 18]. Together, these approaches suggest that combining probabilistic sequence modelling with learned representations can improve robustness and capture richer temporal patterns [15, 17, 18]. Despite this promise, systematic evaluation remains limited in settings where generalisation and ground truth control are central [ 19]. To the best of current knowledge, recent latent state approaches have not been systematically evaluated on synthetic datasets that allow precise control over ground truth sleep dynamics, or for their ability to detect subtle substages of sleep [ 15, 17, 18]. Addressing these gaps is essential for turning unsupervised sleep staging into a dependable scientiﬁc instrument that supports scalable, reproducible analysis across laboratories and subjects. This thesis addresses these gaps through a validation ladder that moves from controlled ground truth to realistic transfer. First, synthetic data based on Mouse Sleep Stage Validation dataset characteristics are used to evaluate how established HMM variants recover known structure under controlled conditions [ 20]. Second, feature extraction and model extensions, including explicit duration modelling and learned representations, are assessed for their ability to improve reliability and predictive performance compared with standard HMM baselines. Third, the resulting frameworks are evaluated for their ability to detect meaningful substages and generalise across individual mice. It describes how time is distributed across stages and how cycles evolve from sleep onset to morning awakening [39]. Human sleep-wake architecture is commonly organised into three broad states. Wake reﬂects sustained arousal and responsiveness. Rapid eye movement sleep (REM) features vivid dreaming, heightened brain activity, and muscle atonia. In contrast, non-rapid eye movement sleep (NREM) covers a continuum from light to deep sleep as physiological activity gradually decreases with depth. In humans, NREM is further divided into three substages, N1, N2, and N3. N1 marks the transition from wakefulness, characterised by slow eye movements and reduced muscle tone. N2 represents light sleep, featuring sleep spindles and K-complexes. Finally, N3, or slow-wave sleep, is the deepest stage of sleep and is associated with slow-wave activity. These states recur in cycles of roughly 90 to 110 minutes, and their proportion and timing vary with age, health status, and context [6]. Using categorical labels gives researchers and clinicians a shared language for scoring and analysis. With common labels, annotation becomes more consistent, clinical communication improves, and population studies gain clearer endpoints. These categories also enable targeted evaluation of outcomes such as learning, mood, and cardiometabolic risk, which can relate di"erently to speciﬁc stages [40]. However, this approach has drawbacks. Stage boundaries are often gradual, so brief transitions can be missed. By compressing rich physiology into discrete bins, categorical labels may obscure within-stage variation, micro-arousals, and regional brain di"erences [ 13]. Fixed scoring rules may also struggle with atypical patterns in certain populations, and the limited set of stage labels may fail to capture transitional states or movement-related artifacts. For these reasons, many analyses complement traditional stage scoring with approaches that preserve temporal dynamics and salient signal features [13]. 2.1.3 EEG and EMG Signals Sleep is typically assessed by recording brain and muscle activity at the same time. Using di"erential ampliﬁers, EEG captures voltage ﬂuctuations generated by coordinated postsynaptic activity in the cortex. Di"erential ampliﬁcation records the summed activity of motor units, providing an index of overall muscle tone as well as brief twitches and movements [43]. Page 9 of 103

The exposition begins with the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) as a baseline for latent-state time-series analysis, followed by its multivariate autoregressive extension to capture within-state temporal structure. The states are not directly observable, which is why they are referred to as hidden. They can only be inferred indirectly through the distribution of the observed data [ 14]. The latent process evolves according to a Markov chain, while each state emits observations through a state-dependent distribution. This separation between latent dynamics and emission modelling provides both interpretability and ﬂexibility, making HMMs a central tool across ﬁelds such as speech recognition, computational biology, and neuroscience. The use of HMMs centres on three methodological tasks, originally formalised by Rabiner [ 14]. Learning involves estimating model parameters from data. Evaluation concerns computing the likelihood of observed data under given parameters. Decod- ing addresses inference of the most probable latent-state sequence explaining the observations. These problems are solved e!ciently by recursive algorithms, commonly referred to as dynamic programming. Concretely, the standard HMM framework comprises the Baum-Welch variant of expectation-maximisation or direct gradient-based optimisation for learning, the forward recursion for evaluation, and the Viterbi algorithm for decoding [1]. In this work, the forward-backward procedure used in EM-based learning is omitted, as parameters are optimised directly via gradient-based maximum likelihood. The HMM methodology is presented in depth. The mathematical formulation is introduced ﬁrst, followed by modelling assumptions and their implications. Learning and inference procedures are then detailed, including both EM and gradient-based approaches, together with computational considerations for multiple sequences and scaling. Regularisation and parameter constraints are discussed before turning to the application of HMMs to sleep-stage data. 3.1.1 F ormulation This report follows the notation and probabilistic formulation of Murphy [ 1], with the structure of dynamic-programming recursions adapted from Rabiner [ 14]. The aim is to reduce sensitivity to subject-speciﬁc scaling di"erences and measurement noise, while retaining the ability to discover structure that is not hard-coded by expert-deﬁned feature choices. The HMM family provides an interpretable probabilistic description of switching dynamics, yet performance depends on the quality and comparability of the obser- vation space. In empirical mouse sleep staging, recordings exhibit inter-individual variability and cross-laboratory shifts in amplitude statistics and spectral baselines, which can destabilise emission modelling and lead to inconsistent state discovery when models are trained directly on raw channels or on ﬁxed engineered features. The expert-driven feature extraction in Section 2.5 improves interpretability, but it introduces two limitations. First, engineered features are not guaranteed to be comparable in scale or distribution across subjects, which can bias state assignments toward subject identity rather than sleep dynamics. Second, features designed around canonical labels such as Awake, NREM, and REM may suppress within-stage structure and reduce sensitivity to substages and transitional dynamics. To address these limitations, observations are mapped to a data-driven latent representation learned from the signals. The design objective is a compact embedding that is comparable across subjects and structured to support discrete stage discov- ery. The results in this thesis also support a complementary view in which sleep is represented as a latent state process expressed through EEG and EMG. In this framing, HMMs provide a proba- bilistic description of state occupancy and switching dynamics. MARHMMs extend this description by allowing each state to capture short range temporal dependence in the observed signals, which in turn supports modelling of within state dynamics that are not well represented by independent emissions. This probabilistic perspective indicates how sequence modelling can support more scalable and reproducible sleep analysis that extends beyond conventional label based staging. 5.8.2 Scientiﬁc Interpretation of Inferred Substages The substages reported in Sections 5.6.4 and 5.7 should be interpreted as plausible structure in the recordings that can guide physiological interpretation and motivate follow up studies. They should not be treated as deﬁnitive or fully generalisable states. Substage boundaries and transition patterns may vary across cohorts, laboratories, and recording conditions, which motivates cross cohort validation and stability assessment under realistic sources of variation. This framing supports careful use of substages as hypotheses about within stage organisation that can be tested in targeted experiments. 5.8.3 Evaluation Anchoring and Limitations of Label Agreement Interpretation and evaluation remain anchored to human annotated labels and to the canonical Awake, NREM, and REM taxonomy. Agreement based metrics favour alignment with existing conventions, which biases model selection towards known structure and limits what can be concluded about novelty in the learned substages. Stronger claims therefore require validation beyond label agreement, including stable replication and links to independent physiological or behavioural markers. This limitation is central when probabilistic models are used to propose additional structure beyond the standard taxonomy . 5.8.4 Reliability Limits and Monitoring Under Distribution Shift Use in high impact settings, including clinical decision support or sensitive human studies, would require stronger evidence of reliability than is targeted in this thesis. In contrast, the downstream temporal model exploits sequential dependencies. This mismatch can limit the ability of the latent space to represent physiologically consistent trajectories, as motivated by the temporal modelling discussion in Section 5.5 and the empirical transition behaviour reported in Section 5.2. A direct extension is to incorporate temporal dynamics into the conditional variational autoencoder prior by replacing the static mixture prior with a multivariate autoregressive prior in latent space, so that latent trajectories become temporally coherent before any state modelling is applied. This approach aligns with established sequential latent variable modelling practice and provides a principled way to encode temporal continuity in the generative model. Success should be deﬁned by improved stability of stage discovery, reduced state collapse, and stronger agreement between the learned latent dynamics and known biological temporal dynamics. 5.9.4 Richer Signal Representations The present analysis focuses on frequency content below a ﬁxed upper bound, which is a pragmatic choice but can omit informative structure carried at higher frequencies, as acknowledged in Section 2.4.1. Sleep also contains transient events with distinctive morphology that may not be fully captured by ﬁxed frequency-domain summaries, par- ticularly when events are brief, non-stationary, or expressed through waveform shape rather than sustained band power, as discussed in Section 5.2.2. Future work should therefore expand the observation representation along two complementary directions. First, the spectral representation should be extended to include higher-frequency content when the recording hardware and ﬁltering support reliable measurement. Second, time-domain inputs should be incorporated to enable end-to-end learning of features that capture transient morphology, including spindle-like activity, and to re- duce reliance on summaries that can be sensitive to acquisition variability. A practical and computationally tractable strategy is to adopt a staged training scheme in which a spectral model is used to initialise training, followed by end-to-end ﬁne-tuning on time-domain windows under the same conditional framework. Both directions should be evaluated through controlled ablations that hold all other preprocessing steps constant, and should report changes in cross-subject separability in the latent space and temporal consistency of the resulting stage sequences, using the same metrics and visual diagnostics as in Section 5.4 and Section 5.4.2. 5.9.5 Reliability Under Noise and Shift The cross-subject and cross-laboratory analyses demonstrate that distribution shift can alter both covariates and dynamics, which implies that deployment on unseen subjects, laboratories, or montages can lead to performance degradation that is not directly observable from the model outputs alone, as established in Section 5.4. A.1.1 Initial Methodological Plan How well do implementations of existing Hidden Markov Models perform on mouse sleep data? To answer this research question, we will generate synthetic mouse electroencephalogram (EEG) sleep data, based on sleep stage characteristics of the Mouse Sleep Staging Validation dataset (MSSV). For the synthetic data, we will evaluate state distinctness using measures such as KL-divergence between state distributions. We will then evaluate how implementations of existing Hidden Markov Models (HMM) and Multivariate Autoregressive Hidden Markov Models (MAR- HMM) perform on synthetic mouse sleep data and the MSSV dataset. To ensure robust performance, we will evaluate multiple initialisation strategies for the model parameters, including random initialisation, k-means-based state assignments, and dimensionality-reduction approaches such as PCA to provide informed starting points. We will test the model’s robustness by introducing controlled noise and modiﬁcations to the synthetic data. Model performance will be quantiﬁed using Normalised Mutual Information (NMI), along with predictive log-likelihood on held-out EEG sequences and consistency across multiple runs. How can feature extractions and model extensions improve the reliability and e"ectiveness of Hidden Markov Models on mouse sleep data? We will explore extensions to the standard HMM framework, such as Hidden Semi-Markov Models (HSMMs) to explicitly model state durations, or variational autoencoder (V AE) embeddings to capture richer latent patterns across channels and model the non-linear, continuous nature of sleep stages. We will evaluate HMMs trained on both raw EEG signals and extracted features that highlight sleep dynamics, including time-domain statistics, standard frequency-band powers, and multichannel correlations or covariances. Using features allows for better signal-to-noise ratio and lower model complexity, whereas raw signals preserve full temporal resolution and non-linear dynamics. This two-track approach will enable a comparative assessment of the two domains, allowing us to understand the impact of feature extraction Page 91 of 103

Characterizing Sleep Patterns by use of Dynamic Unsupervised Machine Learning Approaches on model performance. Model performance will be quantiﬁed using Normalised Mutual Information (NMI) and predictive log-likelihood on held-out EEG sequences. We will additionally assess consistency across multiple runs, accuracy of predicted state durations, and sensitivity to noise for both raw and feature-driven models, allowing us to identify which approach yields more reliable and robust sleep pattern representations. To contextualise our proposed models, we will compare them against state-of-the-art (SOTA) models for sleep staging such as AE-HMM, TDE-HMM, or V AE-HMM. This comparison will quantify improvements in reliability, robustness, and the ability to capture subtle sleep patterns. How well does our proposed model handle variability in mouse sleep, including substages, and to what extent can it generalise to human sleep patterns? We will investigate how well our proposed model can capture novel or subtle sleep patterns in the MSSV dataset, including substages and transitions that may not be fully captured by existing HMM approaches. Substage signiﬁcance will be assessed through state distinctness metrics, temporal consistency of predicted durations, and stability of latent representations across multiple runs. We will also ensure that predicted states are well represented in the data, avoiding collapse of multiple states onto the same pattern. Expert evaluation will complement these quantitative measures to ensure that identiﬁed patterns are biologically meaningful. Next, we will examine generalisation across mice by assessing whether the model captures consistent sleep patterns in subjects not seen during training and how robust these representations are to individual di"erences. Finally, we will test the model on a human EEG dataset with far more channels and complex sleep architecture, assessing its ability to extract meaningful sleep states, handle richer dynamics, and maintain robustness across species. Characterizing Sleep Patterns by use of Dynamic Unsupervised Machine Learning Approaches A.6 Expert Interview Summary with Birgitte Kornum This appendix provides a structured summary of the expert interview with Birgitte Kornum from the Kornum Lab at the Department of Neuroscience, University of Copenhagen. The summary documents the main observations and the methodological considerations raised during the interview, which were used to support the qualitative assessment reported in Section 5.7.3. This material is included to ensure transparency and traceability of the expert input used in the biological interpretation of the inferred substages. A.6.1 Interview Summary Substages beyond the classic three stage model The expert noted that feature sets based on established sleep markers, such as theta to delta ratios and EMG power, tend to reinforce the classic Wake, NREM, and REM partition, which can make solutions with more than three states appear unlikely due to feature bias. In contrast, representation learning was considered better suited to revealing additional structure because learned features can expose meaningful states that are not explicitly encoded by expert selected metrics. In the present results, the most compelling additions beyond the three macro stages were transition like states, particularly for Wake to NREM and NREM to REM boundaries, and an intermediate pattern in which delta decreases without a su!ciently strong theta increase to resemble full REM. The expert also emphasised that the aim is not to identify an optimal number of substages in isolation, since adding a class can cause other states to disappear or merge, but rather to identify which states are stable, repeatable, and biologically interpretable. On this basis, solutions with approximately seven substages were judged most informative in the present runs because they preserved both transition types, whereas larger solutions sometimes collapsed these distinctions. Model performance and stability across baselines and the V AE Baseline unsupervised models such as HMM and MAR-HMM were considered conceptually appropriate but practically fragile, since increasing the number of substates often led to collapse or inconsistent separation unless extensive tuning was applied. Low dimensional projections based on expert features and PCA were described as showing substantial overlap between classes, which suggests limited separability beyond the three principal stages under ﬁxed feature representations. By comparison, the V AE was assessed as producing clearer separation of core macro stages and more coherent additional structure, which supported the view that representation learning was the most promising direction for robust substage discovery under the present data and pipeline. Artefacts, outliers, and risks to interpretability A central concern was that high conﬁdence model assignments can still correspond to substages that are not biologically real, particularly when the model uses a class to absorb recording noise or residual artefacts. The expert highlighted the risk of confusing true substages with outliers, artefacts, or equipment related structure, even after labelled artefact epochs Page 101 of 103

Characterizing Sleep Patterns by use of Dynamic Unsupervised Machine Learning Approaches are removed and extreme values are clipped. Small substages were not dismissed automatically, since even one to two percent prevalence can correspond to thousands of epochs, but such classes were considered to require careful scrutiny and independent checks. It was further noted that some substages may reﬂect behavioural variants of wakefulness, such as grooming or active wake, which can plausibly manifest as di"erent EEG and EMG mixtures. A recurring atypical EMG rhythm or high frequency component was identiﬁed as an example of a pattern that could reﬂect physiology such as cardio respiratory activity or system noise, and which therefore warrants targeted investigation. Biological plausibility checks considered most informative Bout length dis- tributions were highlighted as an important biological sanity check because transition states are expected to have short bouts, while stable stages should persist longer, and REM can exhibit longer bouts than brief boundary dynamics. Transition matrices were also considered a strong validation tool because biologically plausible states should show logical temporal organisation, while artefact related states may appear as brief excursions that rapidly return to dominant states. Together, these checks were viewed as e"ective for distinguishing stable substages from transient boundary dynamics and for ﬂagging candidate artefact clusters. Data scale and generalisation across mice and laboratories The expert suggested that conditioning on mouse identity was a sensible strategy for improving generalisation, and that extending conditioning to laboratory identity could further reduce lab speciﬁc biases when combining cohorts. Increasing the number of mice and including data from multiple laboratories, rather than restricting analysis to a single high quality subset, was described as a likely major lever for improving both stability and interpretability of inferred substages. This recommendation reﬂects the view that robust biological interpretation ultimately depends on demonstrating repeatability across recording settings and cohorts. Stronger validation of candidate substages The expert recommended vali- dating candidate substages by returning to raw contextual evidence, particularly video, to conﬁrm behavioural correlates such as grooming versus quiet wake and to distinguish genuine substates from artefacts. Transition matrix motifs and bout length distributions were proposed as systematic acceptance criteria for deciding whether a discovered substage should be treated as biologically meaningful or as noise absorption. Artefact handling was also identiﬁed as an area for reﬁnement, either by explicitly including artefact epochs as a dedicated class or by modelling artefacts more directly to assess whether the model separates them cleanly from physiological states. Inputs and preprocessing experiments The expert noted that frequency domain representations may attenuate time local EEG phenomena such as spindles, which can limit sensitivity to microstructure within NREM. A comparison between frequency domain inputs and time domain training was suggested as a route to recovering such Page 102 of 103
