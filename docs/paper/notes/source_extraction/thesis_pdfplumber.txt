Characterizing Sleep Patterns by use of Dynamic Unsupervised Machine Learning Approaches Andreas Holmer Bigom & Oliver Rosbæk Elmgreen MSc Thesis, DTU Compute Supervisors: Morten Mørup & Birgitte Rahbek Kornum February 2026

Abstract This report evaluates dynamic unsupervised sequence models for characterising mouse sleep stages from electroencephalography (EEG) and electromyography (EMG), with emphasis on transfer to unseen subjects. Controlled synthetic experiments validate optimisation behaviour and latent state recovery, and show that when stage information is primarily temporal, autoregressive emissions are required for discrimination. Models are then assessed on Mouse Sleep Staging Validation recordings using subject level fitting and leave one subject out population prediction on a curated subset of ten mice, with agreement quantified by Normalised Mutual Information (NMI) against expert Wake, non- rapideyemovementsleep(NREM),andrapideyemovementsleep(REM)labels. Direct time domain modelling with Hidden Markov models and multivariate autoregressive variants shows negligible population level agreement. Expert engineered spectral and amplitude features restore meaningful unsupervised alignmentbut retain a generalisation gap underleaveone subjectout prediction, reaching population NMI of about 0.51. The most robust transfer is achieved by subject conditioned representation learning, where a conditional Gaussian mixturevariationalautoencoderpreservespopulationpredictiveperformanceand yieldsthestrongestagreementwhenitssubjectconditionedmixturepriorisused directlyforprediction,reachingpopulationNMIofabout0.70. Acomplementary substage analysis shows that the cGMVAE mixture prior supports stable finer grained states that preserve the Wake, NREM, and REM organisation while refining transitions. These results indicate that subject conditioned latent clustering captures transferable sleep stage structure, enabling robust cross subject staging even before explicit temporal modelling. i

Characterizing Sleep Patterns by use of Dynamic Unsupervised Machine Learning Approaches 1 Introduction Sleep staging in the mouse o"ers a direct and quantitative lens on how brain states unfold over time, yet automated pipelines often lose reliability when applied to a new subject. This report assesses dynamic unsupervised sequence models that infer latent organisation from physiological recordings and examines whether the learned structure transfers robustly across subjects while remaining consistent with expert annotations. The analysis further evaluates whether the latent states admit stable and interpretable substages, thereby motivating the methodological and experimental developments presented throughout the remainder of the report. 1.1 Motivation Sleep is a major determinant of brain and systemic health, shaping processes that include memory consolidation and immune regulation [2, 3]. Deviations in sleep archi- tecture are sensitive indicators of neurological, psychiatric, and metabolic disorders [4, 5]. Robust methods that characterise sleep stage structure therefore support both mechanistic neuroscience and translational work that aims to connect physiology to measurable outcomes. Progress in these areas depends on more than total sleep time. Evidence suggests that learning and memory consolidation, mood regulation, and cardiometabolic risk relate to sleep composition and stage structure rather than to total sleep time alone [2, 5]. Sleep is also inherently temporal, since its architecture reflects how stages persist, fragment, and transition across cycles [6]. Methods that preserve stage dynamics and transitions therefore provide a quantitative signature of the processes that regulate sleep and wakefulness [7]. Mousesleepo"ersawellcontrolledexperimentalmodelforstudyingbrainrhythms and sleep regulation. Shared molecular pathways and conserved neural circuits enable findings from mice to inform hypotheses that can later be evaluated in humans [8]. Mouse studies can be scaled to larger cohorts at reasonable cost, and invasive recordings and targeted manipulations are feasible under controlled conditions [8, 9]. These properties make mouse sleep datasets an attractive foundation for building quantitative tools that can support mechanistic discovery. At the same time, murine sleep introduces modelling challenges that reward principled dynamic methods. It describes how time is distributed across stages and how cycles evolve from sleep onset to morning awakening [39]. Human sleep-wake architecture is commonly organised into three broad states. In humans, NREM is further divided into three substages, N1, N2, and N3. N1 marks the transition from wakefulness, characterised by slow eye movements and reduced muscle tone. These states recur in cycles of roughly 90 to 110 minutes, and their proportion and timing vary with age, health status, and context [6]. The states are not directly observable, which is why they are referred to as hidden. They can only be inferred indirectly through the distribution of the observed data [14]. The latent process evolves according to a Markov chain, while each state emits observations through a state-dependent distribution. This separation between latent dynamics and emission modelling provides both interpretability and flexibility, making HMMs a central tool across fields such as speech recognition, computational biology, and neuroscience. The use of HMMs centres on three methodological tasks, originally formalised by Rabiner [14]. Learning involves estimating model parameters from data. Evaluation concerns computing the likelihood of observed data under given parameters. Decod- ing addresses inference of the most probable latent-state sequence explaining the observations. These problems are solved e!ciently by recursive algorithms, commonly referred to as dynamic programming. Concretely, the standard HMM framework comprises the Baum-Welch variant of expectation-maximisation or direct gradient-based optimisation for learning, the forward recursion for evaluation, and the Viterbi algorithm for decoding [1]. In this work, the forward-backward procedure used in EM-based learning is omitted, as parameters are optimised directly via gradient-based maximum likelihood. The HMM methodology is presented in depth. The mathematical formulation is introduced first, followed by modelling assumptions and their implications. Learning and inference procedures are then detailed, including both EM and gradient-based approaches, together with computational considerations for multiple sequences and scaling. Regularisation and parameter constraints are discussed before turning to the application of HMMs to sleep-stage data. 3.1.1 Formulation This report follows the notation and probabilistic formulation of Murphy [1], with the structure of dynamic-programming recursions adapted from Rabiner [14]. First, probabilistic regularisation commonly introduces Bayesian priors on model parameters. A complementary approach is variance flooring, which constrains each covariance matrix to maintain a small minimum variance during training. Second, structural constraints on the emission distributions and transition dy- namics, such as enforcing diagonal or tied Gaussian covariances, reduce the number of free parameters and improve numerical conditioning. Bounds on self-transition probabilities serve a role similar to the sticky prior by limiting state fragmentation and preventing the model from splitting persistent states into many short-lived micro-states. The HMM family provides an interpretable probabilistic description of switching dynamics, yet performance depends on the quality and comparability of the obser- vation space. In empirical mouse sleep staging, recordings exhibit inter-individual variability and cross-laboratory shifts in amplitude statistics and spectral baselines, which can destabilise emission modelling and lead to inconsistent state discovery when models are trained directly on raw channels or on fixed engineered features. Across controlled synthetic settings, the modelling pipeline behaved as expected and demonstrated strong recovery of the ground truth state structure, which validates the implementation under favourable separability conditions. A further limitation is that the current VAE representation is learned per epoch without explicit temporal modelling, which may attenuate autoregressive structure that could be informative for downstream state dynamics. Overall, Sections 5.1 to 5.4 provide a quantitative benchmark of model agreement, reliability, and generalisation, and the next section builds on these results through a qualitative analysis of sleep dynamics and the biological interpretation of potential sleep substages. In contrast, the downstream temporal model exploits sequential dependencies. This mismatch can limit the ability of the latent space to represent physiologically consistent trajectories, as motivated by the temporal modelling discussion in Section 5.5 and the empirical transition behaviour reported in Section 5.2. A direct extension is to incorporate temporal dynamics into the conditional variational autoencoder prior by replacing the static mixture prior with a multivariate autoregressive prior in latent space, so that latent trajectories become temporally coherent before any state modelling is applied. This approach aligns with established sequential latent variable modelling practice and provides a principled way to encode temporal continuity in the generative model. Success should be defined by improved stability of stage discovery, reduced state collapse, and stronger agreement between the learned latent dynamics and known biological temporal dynamics. 5.9.4 Richer Signal Representations The present analysis focuses on frequency content below a fixed upper bound, which is a pragmatic choice but can omit informative structure carried at higher frequencies, as acknowledged in Section 2.4.1. Sleep also contains transient events with distinctive morphologythatmaynotbefullycapturedbyfixedfrequency-domainsummaries,par- ticularly when events are brief, non-stationary, or expressed through waveform shape rather than sustained band power, as discussed in Section 5.2.2. Future work should therefore expand the observation representation along two complementary directions. First, the spectral representation should be extended to include higher-frequency content when the recording hardware and filtering support reliable measurement. Second, time-domain inputs should be incorporated to enable end-to-end learning of features that capture transient morphology, including spindle-like activity, and to re- duce reliance on summaries that can be sensitive to acquisition variability. A practical and computationally tractable strategy is to adopt a staged training scheme in which a spectral model is used to initialise training, followed by end-to-end fine-tuning on time-domain windows under the same conditional framework. Both directions should be evaluated through controlled ablations that hold all other preprocessing steps constant, and should report changes in cross-subject separability in the latent space and temporal consistency of the resulting stage sequences, using the same metrics and visual diagnostics as in Section 5.4 and Section 5.4.2. 5.9.5 Reliability Under Noise and Shift The cross-subject and cross-laboratory analyses demonstrate that distribution shift can alter both covariates and dynamics, which implies that deployment on unseen subjects, laboratories, or montages can lead to performance degradation that is not directly observable from the model outputs alone, as established in Section 5.4. A.1.1 Initial Methodological Plan How well do implementations of existing Hidden Markov Models perform on mouse sleep data? To answer this research question, we will generate synthetic mouse electroencephalogram (EEG) sleep data, based on sleep stage characteristics of the Mouse Sleep Staging Validation dataset (MSSV). For the synthetic data, we will evaluate state distinctness using measures such as KL-divergence between state distributions. We will then evaluate how implementations of existing Hidden Markov Models (HMM) and Multivariate Autoregressive Hidden Markov Models (MAR- HMM) perform on synthetic mouse sleep data and the MSSV dataset. To ensure robust performance, we will evaluate multiple initialisation strategies for the model parameters, including random initialisation, k-means-based state assignments, and dimensionality-reduction approaches such as PCA to provide informed starting points. We will test the model’s robustness by introducing controlled noise and modifications to the synthetic data. Model performance will be quantified using Normalised Mutual Information (NMI), along with predictive log-likelihood on held-out EEG sequences and consistency across multiple runs. How can feature extractions and model extensions improve the reliability and e"ectiveness of Hidden Markov Models on mouse sleep data? We will explore extensions to the standard HMM framework, such as Hidden Semi-Markov Models (HSMMs) to explicitly model state durations, or variational autoencoder (VAE) embeddings to capture richer latent patterns across channels and model the non-linear, continuous nature of sleep stages. We will evaluate HMMs trained on both raw EEG signals and extracted features that highlight sleep dynamics, including time-domain statistics, standard frequency-band powers, and multichannel correlations or covariances. Using features allows for better signal-to-noise ratio and lower model complexity, whereas raw signals preserve full temporal resolution and non-linear dynamics. This two-track approach will enable a comparative assessment of the two domains, allowing us to understand the impact of feature extraction Page 91 of 103

Model performance will be quantified using Normalised Mutual Information (NMI) and predictive log-likelihood on held-out EEG sequences. We will additionally assess consistency across multiple runs, accuracy of predicted state durations, and sensitivity to noise for both raw and feature-driven models, allowing us to identify which approach yields more reliable and robust sleep pattern representations. To contextualise our proposed models, we will compare them against state-of-the-art (SOTA) models for sleep staging such as AE-HMM, TDE-HMM, or VAE-HMM. This comparison will quantify improvements in reliability, robustness, and the ability to capture subtle sleep patterns. How well does our proposed model handle variability in mouse sleep, including substages, and to what extent can it generalise to human sleep patterns? We will investigate how well our proposed model can capture novel or subtle sleep patterns in the MSSV dataset, including substages and transitions that may not be fully captured by existing HMM approaches. Substage significance will be assessed through state distinctness metrics, temporal consistency of predicted durations, and stability of latent representations across multiple runs. We will also ensure that predicted states are well represented in the data, avoiding collapse of multiple states onto the same pattern. Expert evaluation will complement these quantitative measures to ensure that identified patterns are biologically meaningful. Next, we will examine generalisation across mice by assessing whether the model captures consistent sleep patterns in subjects not seen during training and how robust these representations are to individual di"erences. Finally, we will test the model on a human EEG dataset with far more channels and complex sleep architecture, assessing its ability to extract meaningful sleep states, handle richer dynamics, and maintain robustness across species. The summary documents the main observations and the methodological considerations raised during the interview, which were used to support the qualitative assessment reported in Section 5.7.3. A.6.1 Interview Summary Substages beyond the classic three stage model Theexpertnotedthatfeature sets based on established sleep markers, such as theta to delta ratios and EMG power, tend to reinforce the classic Wake, NREM, and REM partition, which can make solutions with more than three states appear unlikely due to feature bias. In contrast, representation learning was considered better suited to revealing additional structure because learned features can expose meaningful states that are not explicitly encoded by expert selected metrics. In the present results, the most compelling additions beyond the three macro stages were transition like states, particularly for Wake to NREM and NREM to REM boundaries, and an intermediate pattern in which delta decreases without a su!ciently strong theta increase to resemble full REM. The expert also emphasised that the aim is not to identify an optimal number of substages in isolation, since adding a class can cause other states to disappear or merge, but rather to identify which states are stable, repeatable, and biologically interpretable. On this basis, solutions with approximately seven substages were judged most informative in the present runs because they preserved both transition types, whereas larger solutions sometimes collapsed these distinctions. Model performance and stability across baselines and the VAE Baseline unsupervised models such as HMM and MAR-HMM were considered conceptually appropriate but practically fragile, since increasing the number of substates often led to collapse or inconsistent separation unless extensive tuning was applied. Low dimensional projections based on expert features and PCA were described as showing substantial overlap between classes, which suggests limited separability beyond the three principal stages under fixed feature representations. By comparison, the VAE was assessed as producing clearer separation of core macro stages and more coherent additional structure, which supported the view that representation learning was the most promising direction for robust substage discovery under the present data and pipeline. Artefacts, outliers, and risks to interpretability A central concern was that high confidence model assignments can still correspond to substages that are not biologically real, particularly when the model uses a class to absorb recording noise or residual artefacts. The expert highlighted the risk of confusing true substages with outliers, artefacts, or equipment related structure, even after labelled artefact epochs Page 101 of 103

Biological plausibility checks considered most informative Bout length dis- tributions were highlighted as an important biological sanity check because transition states are expected to have short bouts, while stable stages should persist longer, and REM can exhibit longer bouts than brief boundary dynamics. Transition matrices were also considered a strong validation tool because biologically plausible states should show logical temporal organisation, while artefact related states may appear as brief excursions that rapidly return to dominant states. Together, these checks were viewed as e"ective for distinguishing stable substages from transient boundary dynamics and for flagging candidate artefact clusters. Data scale and generalisation across mice and laboratories The expert suggested that conditioning on mouse identity was a sensible strategy for improving generalisation, and that extending conditioning to laboratory identity could further reduce lab specific biases when combining cohorts. Increasing the number of mice and including data from multiple laboratories, rather than restricting analysis to a single high quality subset, was described as a likely major lever for improving both stability and interpretability of inferred substages. This recommendation reflects the view that robust biological interpretation ultimately depends on demonstrating repeatability across recording settings and cohorts. Stronger validation of candidate substages The expert recommended vali- dating candidate substages by returning to raw contextual evidence, particularly video, to confirm behavioural correlates such as grooming versus quiet wake and to distinguish genuine substates from artefacts. Transition matrix motifs and bout length distributions were proposed as systematic acceptance criteria for deciding whether a discovered substage should be treated as biologically meaningful or as noise absorption. Artefact handling was also identified as an area for refinement, either by explicitly including artefact epochs as a dedicated class or by modelling artefacts more directly to assess whether the model separates them cleanly from physiological states.
